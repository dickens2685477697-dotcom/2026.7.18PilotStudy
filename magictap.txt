Magic-Tap: A Kinematics-Driven Virtual Hand
Selection Technique in AR/VR
Ruyang Yu, Yixuan Liu, Zijian Wu, and Tao Luo
Abstract—This study explores the design of a selection tech-
nique in virtual environments leveraging kinematic data derived
from hand movements. We first identified the intrinsic challenges
of virtual hand selection techniques, particularly in complex
settings, including Accidental Selection , Slow Selection , Failed
Selection, and Fragmented Selection . To mitigate these issues,
we introduce Magic-Tap, a selection technique that ascertains
the trigger of an object based on real-time variations in virtual
hand acceleration and speed, seamlessly integrating the pointing
and triggering processes without requiring explicit triggering
signals. The parameter settings of Magic-Tap were fine-tuned
through Study One, ameliorating its trigger rate, error rate, and
trigger time. Furthermore, we compared Magic-Tap with three
conventional virtual hand selection techniques (Touch, Dwell-
Time, and Pinch) in Study Two. The results indicate that the
task completion time of Magic-Tap is comparable to Touch in all
situations while exhibiting an error rate as low as Dwell-Time
and Pinch.
Index Terms—Virtual hand interaction, Selection Techniques,
Kinematics-Driven, AR/VR
I. INTRODUCTION
Object selection is a fundamental interaction in augmented
and virtual reality, often serving as the initial step for various
interactions [1]. Over the past three decades, HCI researchers
have proposed numerous pointing facilitation techniques suit-
able for different selection environments. The extensive body
of work on object selection in 3D environments can be
classified into two categories [2]: Virtual Hand (i.e., selection
by direct touching) and Ray Casting (i.e., selection by virtual
rays) metaphors. Both categories face difficulties when deal-
ing with complex selection environments [3]. While current
research primarily focuses on ray casting selection techniques
[4], virtual hand selection techniques, considered to be the
most natural and practical [5], have been scarcely explored in
more complex selection settings.
Virtual hand selection techniques have intrinsic challenges
due to head-worn displays’ persistent tracking of the finger
position. Conventionally, a selection is initiated when the
virtual hand spatially aligns with the target, occurring either
at the moment the virtual hand enters or exits the target
area. Although intuitive, such selection methods may lead
to the unintended activation of incorrect targets in complex
environments [6], a phenomenon we term Accidental Selection
(Figure 1a). To mitigate this issue, many researchers have
utilized dwell as an implicit gesture for users to convey their

intent to trigger. While such mechanisms can notably reduce
accidental selections, they may lead to prolonged trigger time
[7], a phenomenon we refer to as Slow Selection (Figure 1b).
Alternatively, incorporating explicit gestures as an additional
triggering condition can minimize delay, but such techniques
usually require binary confirmation (e.g., pinching [8] or
circling [9]), which fragments the pointing and triggering
processes of the selection—a phenomenon we refer to as
Fragmented Selection (Figure 1c). Furthermore, adding either
implicit or explicit conditions may lead to trigger failures if
the conditions are not met due to minor errors or interruptions,
necessitating a reattempt of the selection task. We refer to this
as Failed Selection (Figure 1b and c).
To address these challenges, we introduce Magic-Tap, a
selection technique that utilizes real-time kinematic data vari-
ations of the finger. Usually, the kinematic data of the finger
significantly differs between intentional and unintentional se-
lections (Figure 2), and Magic-Tap utilizes this difference to
discern the user’s intent of selection. In Study One, we col-
lected finger kinematic data during the selection process and
utilized this data to calibrate the parameter settings of Magic-
Tap, effectively alleviating the challenges mentioned earlier. In
Study Two, we compared Magic-Tap’s performance with con-
ventional virtual hand selection techniques, i.e., Touch, Dwell-
Time, and Pinch. Our main contributions are: (1) We propose
Magic-tap as an alternative selection technique that naturally
integrates the pointing and triggering processes. The results of
Study Two indicate that Magic-Tap can maintain a relatively
short task completion time while ensuring a significantly low
error rate. (2) We offered recommendations for optimizing
Magic-Tap’s parameter configurations in various contexts.
II. RELATED WORK
We began by reviewing previous research on device-free
selection techniques in AR/VR, narrowing our focus to the
virtual hand metaphor and outlining its challenges. We then
examined techniques using kinematic data to alleviate these
challenges and proposed a viable approach. Finally, we dis-
tilled important factors from earlier studies to inform the
design of our technique.

are widely adopted by commercial head-mounted displays
(HMDs) as standard methods for 3D interface interactions
without extra hardware support [10]. However, different tech-
niques vary in accuracy, speed, immersion, and usability. The

B. Hand-based Selection Techniques
Virtual hand and Ray Casting are two primary metaphors for
hand-based selection techniques [19]. The classical implemen-
tation of the virtual hand metaphor entails a direct mapping
of the user’s hand movements to the motions of the virtual
hand, capitalizing on the innate human familiarity with hand-
based interactions in daily life. In the case of ray casting, users
can select various objects by manipulating a designated ray
emitted from the fingertip or wrist, facilitating the selection
of distant objects. Feuchtner’s study [20] indicates that virtual
pointing facilitates more effective and accurate selection than
virtual hand. However, Yu [21] compared the metaphors of
ray casting and virtual hand under different density levels and
selection areas, arriving at a different conclusion. The results
indicated that both metaphors offer similar performance and
result in nearly identical error rates. In this context, we narrow
our research focus to the virtual hand metaphor to enhance its
performance.
Although the virtual hand is promising, it lacks a cohesive
mechanism for pointing and triggering selections [22]. In
current applications, the spatial coupling of the virtual hand
with the target is the most common triggering mechanism [23],
praised for its intuitiveness and efficiency. Even so, compared
to mouse movement, the throughput of virtual hand techniques
remains lower ( 2.7bps vs. 4.2bps) while increasing the risk
of Accidental Selections [24]. A common solution is adding
dwell time, which triggers selection after a short pause ( 150-
1500ms) over a target [25]. However, it experiences a trade-off
between the duration of this pause (and overall target acquisi-
tion times) and the incidence of Midas Touch, or unintentional
target activations during exploration or natural pauses in mo-
tion [26]. Some researchers have employed pinching [8], [27],
drawing circles [9], and other gestures as explicit confirmation
signals for triggering. While dwell time and additional gestures
can reduce the risk of accidental selections, these disrupt the
continuity of interaction, potentially leading to Slow Selection
or Fragmented Selections. Dube et al. [7] further confirmed
that dwell time (800ms) and pinch gestures result in reduced
throughput, with values of 1.73bps and 2.09bps, respectively.
Moreover, these mechanisms might induce the Heisenberg
effect of Spatial Interaction [28], increasing the risk of Failed
Selection. Therefore, we recognized that devising appropriate
triggering mechanisms is pivotal in mitigating these challenges
for a virtual hand selection technique.
C. Utilizing Kinematic Data for Triggering Mechanisms
Recently, many researchers [29]–[31] have delved into a
promising technique through motion matching — targets move
in regular patterns, and head or eye motions are correlated
against these changes. A selection is triggered when a sim-
ilarity threshold is exceeded. In the comparative study by
Esteves et al. [9], it was noted that while motion matching
strikes a balance between speed and accuracy, it also imposes
behavioral constraints on users, requiring them to mimic
motion trajectories passively rather than interact naturally with
the target.
Moreover, kinematic data provided valuable information for
Endpoint and Target Prediction. Researchers used Kinematic
Template Matching (KTM) [32], [33], Regression-Based Ex-
trapolation [34], and Classical Kinematics [35] methods to
predict the future locations or potential targets when pointing.
These efforts have made significant contributions to improving
pointing efficiency and reducing accidental activation during
the process. However, human intent inherently contains uncer-
tainty and variability [36], significantly affecting the alignment
between past actions and final decisions [37]. This leaves
substantial room for research into identifying the appropriate
timing for final activation. Therefore, we aim to explore the in-
tegration of unique kinematic data features of the virtual hand
into the real-time triggering mechanism to better determine
activation.

D. Important Factors
To develop an intuitive and efficient virtual hand selection
technique, it is essential to distill the Important Factors
from previous research on object selection in AR/VR [2]:
(F1) Seamlessness: the technique should facilitate a seamless
transition from pointing to confirming the selection, mirroring
natural selection behavior to prevent Fragmented Selection
[38]; (F2) Easy-to-trigger : the primary concern during the
selection task should be the ease of triggering to minimize
Failed Selection [39]; (F3) Error-proofing: a cancellation or
prevention mechanism should be in place, allowing users to
promptly cancel or prevent the triggering process in cases of
unintentional selection or ambiguous intent, avoiding Acciden-
tal Selection [7]; (F4) Temporal Efficiency: when successfully
triggering a selection, the technique should efficiently trigger
the selection, avoiding Slow Selection [2]; (F5) Workload: the
technique should minimize the mental and physical burden on
users, especially in continuous selection tasks [2].
III. MAGIC-TAP
We first outlined the target applications for Magic-Tap.
Then, we introduced the design of Magic-Tap and detailed
its algorithm in Figure 3. The code is openly available at [40].
A. Target Applications
Our exploration of selection techniques is geared towards
typical AR/VR applications, such as immersive virtual cock-
pits [3]. These applications necessitate swift and accurate
selection of virtual or real objects using a virtual or real
hand. Furthermore, many applications, such as interactive data
visualization or on-site CAD, may require the selection of
a specific target within a fully immersive, relatively dense,
and complex environment. The options for selection in these
applications are typically situated within arm’s reach or at
locations accessible through short movements. Additionally,
to comply with the principles of user-friendly interfaces, these
options generally do not overlap or obscure each other in the
line of sight. Given that the virtual hand is not well-suited for
operation in occluded environments [21], we temporarily do
not consider such scenarios in this study.
B. Technique Design
Utilizing Kinematic Data. We use the index fingertip (as
shown in Figure 2a) as a triggering device for our technique,
widely regarded as the most intuitive instrument [42]. Two
types of kinematic data, namely speed (indicative of magnitude
without direction) and acceleration are extracted in three
dimensions (x, y, and z) from existing HMDs that support hand
tracking [43], [44]. Speed, the magnitude of the first derivative
of position with respect to time, displays a stable trend, mak-
ing it suitable for more deliberate indications. Acceleration,
describing how quickly speed increases or decreases, serves
as an earlier and more sensitive indicator of finger motion.
Consequently, by merging the sensitivity of acceleration with
the stability of speed, we formulated a selection mechanism:

utilizing finger acceleration for Pre-trigger activation and
speed for Pre-trigger cancellation. Employing this foundation
for Magic-Tap’s trigger mechanism could reduce activation
time and enhance the precision of the mechanism’s decision-
making process [45].
Triggering Methods. Trigger Method 1 constitutes the
central triggering mechanism of Magic-Tap. Generally, a sig-
nificant deceleration of the finger (i.e., acceleration is less
than 0) within the target signals a genuine intent to trigger
the target (an example is shown in Figure 2b). Therefore,
when the finger acceleration (FA) drops below α, Trigger
Method 1 activate Pre-trigger (as seen in Figure 3b). This
method could effectively prevent accidental selections caused
by Glide-past gestures (refer to Figure 2c). Trigger Method
2 is a supplementary triggering mechanism for Magic-Tap.
If the user’s finger enters an object at a very slow speed
and remains inside, the fluctuation of acceleration might be
minimal, making Trigger Method 1 unreliable. To address this
issue, Trigger Method 2 activates the Pre-trigger when the
finger speed (FS) falls below β, indicative of a near stationary
state (refer to Figure 3b).
Cancellation Mechanism. We observed that in some cases,
users quickly retract their fingers upon realizing they’ve en-
tered the wrong object (Figure 2d). Although this pattern
typically indicates an unintentional selection, the deceleration
data upon finger entry unexpectedly meets the thresholds for
both Trigger Method 1 and Trigger Method 2 , potentially
leading to false triggers. Therefore, we integrated a Pre-trigger
containing two functional modules: Switch State (SS) and
Update Timer (UT) (Figure 3c) to cancel activation caused
by the Snap-back gesture in time. The complete algorithm for
Magic-Tap can be found in Figure 3.
IV. STUDY ONE: CALIBRATING MAGIC-TAP
Fine-tuning the parameters is crucial for optimal perfor-
mance. Initially, we gathered kinematic data through Exper-
iment 1 to establish a reasonable testing range for pertinent
parameters. Subsequently, we evaluated user performance and
satisfaction in Experiment 2 to pinpoint the most harmonious
combination of parameters, which would later serve as a
benchmark compared with traditional techniques.
A. Experiment 1: Collecting Kinematic Data
1) Participants and Apparatus: Through social media, we
recruited 15 participants (7 females, 8 males, mean age 24.8
years, SD=4.021) with multidisciplinary backgrounds (includ-
ing intelligent manufacturing, materials, chemical engineering,
computer science, design, etc.) from local universities. Among
them, 8 participants were considered experts, with an average
of more than 5 hours of AR/VR usage per week, while the
remaining 7 participants were novices with no prior AR/VR
experience. The selection criteria for participants included
AR/VR experience, no motor disabilities, and their vision had
to be either normal or corrected to normal. All participants
were right-handed and used their dominant hand for testing.
They remained seated throughout the task (Figure 4b). Partic-
ipants were paid about 10 dollars per hour.
The experiment used the Oculus Quest Pro HMD, with
a resolution of 1800 × 1920 per eye. The HMD’s built-in
cameras enabled 6 degrees of freedom (DoF) hand tracking
and finger configuration. The hand used an absolute mapping
to control the virtual hand for input and selection. The
system ran on a laptop with an i9-13900HX processor and
an NVIDIA RTX4060 graphics card and was developed in
Unity3D (version 2020.3.37f1c1). The HMD was connected
to the laptop during the experiment, and participants’ experi-
mental processes were monitored in real-time via Quest Link.
2) Design, Task, and Procedure: We aimed to collect and
analyze the kinematic data of the participant’s index fingertip
during natural clicking actions across three stages: (1) Entry
Stage: characterized by the fluctuation threshold of the fin-
ger deceleration upon entering the target; (2) Halting Stage :
wherein the device presents kinematic data corresponding to
a stationary finger; and (3) Departure Stage: marked by the
acceleration data as the finger withdraws from the target.
Before the formal experiment, participants were required
to attend a 15-minute training session. In the experiment,
participants were directed to click a blue ball (with a diameter
of 60mm) 530mm away (the comfortable clicking distance
calculated as 0.736 times the average arm length [46]). The
ball’s transparency decreased upon the finger’s entry, serving
as visual feedback. Participants were then instructed to hold
their finger steady for 3 seconds before being prompted to
withdraw. This process (Figure 4a) was repeated 10 times,
with the entire experiment lasting approximately 25 minutes
for each participant.
3) Result: Upon examining the data, we observed that dur-
ing the Halting Stage, the acceleration (Figure 4c) and speed
(Figure 4d) values typically remain below 3 m/s2 and 0.1 m/s,
respectively. We used these values as the criteria to segment
different stages. Subsequently, we used the interquartile range
method to remove outliers. In total, we collected 1, 137 data
points in the Entry Stage (with a 7% outlier removal rate),
22, 050 data points in the Halting Stage (with a 5.8% outlier
removal rate), and 458 data points in the Departure Stage (with
a 3% outlier removal rate). We compiled statistics on both
speed and acceleration for each stage, categorizing the data
into three types: Peak Average, Trough Average, and Overall
Average, and details can be found in Table I .
Based on the mechanism of Magic-Tap, we considered the
Trough Average of acceleration during the Entry Stage as
the Acceleration reference value for Trigger method 1 (i.e.,
−10.007m/s2 as α value). This value indicates the user’s
intention to remain within the target, distinguishing it from
the Glide-past gesture. The Overall Average of speed during
the Departure Stage was taken as the Exit Speed reference
value of Cancellation Mechanism (i.e., 0.339m/s as γ value),
facilitating an earlier detection of cancellation intentions com-
pared to utilizing the Average Peak speed. Moreover, we
detected noise in both acceleration and speed during the
Halting Stage (Figure 4c and d), which was also considered
in the subsequent parameter configuration to enhance the
technique’s performance.

B. Experiment 2: Identifying Appropriate Configurations for
Magic-Tap
1) Participants and Apparatus: We recruited 15 partici-
pants (4 females, 11 males, mean age 25.2 years, SD=3.668).
8 were considered experts, and 7 were novices. Notably, due
to our strict requirement for experts, the 6 experts in this
experiment were the same as in Experiment 1, with all novices
being new to our studies. All participants were right-handed,
used their dominant hand for testing, and remained seated
throughout the task. The recruitment channels, selection crite-
ria, AR/VR experience criteria for participants, compensation,
and apparatus were consistent with Experiment 1.
2) Design: We employed a 3 × 3 within-subjects design
with two independent variables: Acceleration ( −3.352m/s2,
−6.680m/s2, −10.007m/s2) and Exit Speed ( 0.128m/s,
0.233m/s, 0.339m/s), resulting in 9 unique combinations.
These values were based on reference data from Experiment 1
(with α = −10.007m/s2 and γ = 0.339m/s). To reduce noise
effects, we selected three equally spaced data points between
these reference values and the overall average acceleration
and speed observed in the Halting Stage. These combinations
were assigned to 9 blue balls serving as trigger conditions,
positioned 537mm away from participants. We also provided
auditory feedback and color-changing balls as visual feedback
for triggering (Figure 5a). In the pilot study, we found Trigger
Method 2 consistently ensured successful activation but was
always slower than Trigger Method 1 (the central triggering
mechanism). Therefore, this experiment honed in exploring the
synergistic impact of the central triggering and cancellation
mechanisms and excluded Trigger Method 2 as it would
interfere with the interpretability of the experimental data.
3) Task and Procedure: Participants were required to use
three gestures ( Deliberate Selection , Glide-past, and Snap-
back as shown in Figure 2) to test 9 combinations. The
experiment consisted of four rounds. In the initial round,
participants exclusively used the Deliberate Selection gesture
to trigger the balls, assessing the likelihood of Failed Selection
and Slow Selection with different combinations. We tracked
the Trigger Rate (total times of trigger / total times of finger
entering balls) and Trigger Time (from the finger entering
the balls until successful triggering). In the subsequent two
rounds, aimed at assessing the risk of accidental selections,
participants were asked to perform Glide-past and Snap-back
gestures, respectively. We tracked the Error Rate (total times
of accidental trigger / total times of finger entering balls). In
the final round, participants could conduct a comprehensive

V. STUDY TWO: COMPARING MAGIC-TAP’S
PERFORMANCE WITH CONVENTIONAL
SELECTION TECHNIQUES
As previously defined, we concentrate on device-free vir-
tual hand selection techniques. We selected three mainstream
Virtual Hand selection techniques in current AR/VR devices
[43], [44] for comparison with Magic-Tap, including Touch,
Dwell-Time, and Pinch. The key distinction between them is
their unique triggering mechanisms. Touch solely relies on the
spatial coupling between the finger and the target. Dwell-Time
builds upon spatial coupling by adding a fixed dwell time
for error-proofing. Pinch involves aligning the target object
with the pinch point created by the thumb and index finger.
This study aimed to evaluate the performance and differences
between Magic-Tap and these techniques.
A. Participants and Apparatus
We recruited 20 participants (10 females, 10 males, mean
age 25.4 years, SD=2.088). 8 were considered experts, 12
were novices, and all participants had not been involved in
our previous experiments. All participants were right-handed,
used their dominant hand for testing, and remained seated
throughout the task. The recruitment channels, selection crite-
ria, AR/VR experience criteria for participants, compensation,
and apparatus were consistent with those used in Study One.
B. Design
We used a 4 × 3 within-subjects design, with four Selection
Techniques (Touch, Dwell-Time, Pinch, and Magic-Tap) and
three Indices of Difficulty (H-ID, M-ID, L-ID, related to the
target size and distance) as the two independent variables. The
target selection task was designed based on the ISO 9241-411

targets would shift between 20% and 100% as fingers entered
or exited their proximity, and auditory feedback, wherein
distinct sounds were used to indicate the triggering of targets
or obstacles.
C. Task and Procedure
We first introduced subjects to the experimental task and
how the technique would be used and provided training on a
similar prototype (about 15 minutes). To mitigate bias from
cumulative experimental experience and variations in target
search time, we pre-informed participants about target distri-
bution and occurrence patterns and provided a brief retraining
before testing each technique. After the official start of the
experiment, 16 red balls were presented to the participants in
a sequence (consistent with the ISO 9241-411 standard [48]),
along with corresponding obstacles, as shown in Figure 7b.
Among them, the first red ball in each task is considered the
Starting Ball, and the system begins recording data only after
it is triggered. Correctly triggering a red ball would change
it to green (Figure 7c), while mistakenly triggering a blue
ball (obstacle) would turn it yellow (Figure 7d). The order of
the four selection techniques, ID tasks, and the starting ball
position were counterbalanced.
We recorded Task Completion Time (from activating the
Starting Ball to triggering the last red ball), Task Error Rate
(number of incorrect triggers of blue balls / 15 target balls),
Throughput, Movement Distance of the fingertip, and Trigger
Time of Magic-Tap as dependent variables. After complet-
ing all tasks with each technique, participants completed
the NASA-TLX and USER EXPERIENCE QUESTIONNAIRE ,
followed by a 5-minute semi-structured interview and asked
participants to rank the techniques based on their overall
preference. Each participant’s experiment lasted approximately
60 minutes. In total, the number of target selections was 3600
(= 20 Participants × 4 Selection Techniques × 3 IDs × 15
Target Selections).

VI. DISCUSSION
In this section, we first discuss the implications of Study
One and recommend optimizing Magic-Tap’s parameter con-
figurations for different scenarios. Moreover, based on the
results of Study Two, we compare Magic-Tap’s unique trigger-
ing mechanism with traditional mechanisms and discuss our
findings.
A. Interpreting Kinematic Data: Implications of Study One
1) Kinematic Data Depiction Under Selection Behavior:
In Experiment 1, the overall trend of the data indicates that
when the user’s finger enters the ball, there is a sharp drop
in both acceleration and speed, followed by a clear signal
upon departure. These patterns provide critical insights for
the design and parameter configuration of Magic-Tap. Notably,
the acceleration and speed did not stabilize at zero when the
finger remained stationary inside the ball. This discrepancy
might be attributed to motor control’s constant [52] and signal-
dependent noise [53]. Incorporating signal processing methods
[54] would be beneficial for further utilization.
2) How Different Parameters Impact Trigger Rate and
Trigger Time: Seow et al. [55] have noted that users perceive
a response as instantaneous if it occurs within 0.1-0.2 second.
In Experiment 2, mean Trigger Time (Figure 6b) showed that
most combinations met this criterion except for -3×0.1, -6×0.1,
and -10×0.1. However, the theoretical shortest trigger time
for Magic-Tap is 0.06s, which was achieved only once in
20,089 attempts. Upon reviewing the raw data, we discovered
that “minor finger jitters” could mistakenly satisfy the Exit
Speed threshold, resulting in the Pre-Trigger being repeatedly
activated and deactivated, leading to extended trigger time or
even trigger failures. These jitters can be explained by the
“Oscillation” phenomenon [56], where pointing movements
naturally result in small oscillatory adjustments as the motor
system attempts to maintain stability. Drawing from the results
of Experiment 2, we identified Key Patterns of utilizing
Kinematic Data in Magic-Tap’s mechanism:
P1: A higher Acceleration threshold (e.g., α = −3m/s2 or
−6m/s2) makes it easier to activate Pre-Trigger than a lower
one (e.g., α = −10m/s2).
P2: Pairing higher Acceleration with higher Exit Speed
(e.g., γ=0.2m/s or 0.3m/s) could mitigate oscillations causing
Pre-Trigger deactivations, thus improving Trigger Rate and
reducing Trigger Time.
P3: In contrast, pairing higher Acceleration with lower Exit
Speed (e.g., γ=0.1m/s) increases Magic-Tap’s sensitivity to fin-
ger oscillations, resulting in frequent Pre-Trigger deactivation
and longer Trigger Time.
Based on these findings, for optimal performance in terms of
Trigger Rate and Trigger Time, we recommend the following
settings: Acceleration Threshold α should be equal or greater
than −6m/s2 (e.g., α = −3m/s2 or −6m/s2), and Exit Speed
γ should be equal or greater than 0.2m/s (e.g., γ = 0 .2m/s or
0.3m/s).
3) The Trade-off Between Error Rate, Trigger Rate, and
Trigger Time: In Experiment 2, all combinations demonstrated
exceptionally low Error Rates for Glide-past gestures, an
advantage stemming from Magic-Tap’s intrinsic mechanism.
However, combinations with a higher Trigger Rate and shorter
Trigger Time also exhibited higher Error Rates for Snap-back
gestures. The main effect analysis indicated that Exit Speed
is the predominant factor in this trade-off. Although a larger
Exit Speed (e.g., γ = 0 .2m/s or 0.3m/s) may reduce the
negative impact of finger oscillations, it also renders Magic-
Tap less sensitive to cancel activation compared to a smaller
Exit Speed (e.g., γ = 0.1m/s). Therefore, we identified that it
is challenging for a single parameter combination to excel in
all three metrics simultaneously. Combinations that perform
exceptionally in two or three aspects tend to underperform in
the remaining aspect(s).
Based on this trade-off, we recommend the -3 ×0.1 combi-
nation for achieving a significantly high Trigger Rate and low
Error Rates for both Glide-past and Snap-back gestures. Al-
though these come at the cost of a slightly longer trigger time
(M=0.242s, SD= 0.024), it is very close to the instantaneous
perceptual threshold, and its overall performance was favored
by all participants in Experiment 2.

R2: For applications like virtual keyboards [58], [59],

keystrokes. Considering this unique behavior in such applica-
tions, we recommend setting the Update Timer to 0 seconds,
effectively disabling Magic-Tap’sCancellation Mechanism for
the Snap-back gestures and shortening the Trigger Time.

than −6m/s2 could still effectively prevent errors caused by
Glide-past gestures.
R3: For critical applications like engineering or healthcare
(e.g., Vuforia Studio [60]), where accuracy is more important
than speed, we recommend the -3×0.1 setting for its balance
of remarkably low Error Rate and latency. However, while
an error-proof mechanism is beneficial, it may not be entirely
adequate for scenarios where the consequences of erroneous
triggering are particularly severe (e.g., permanently deleting
files). Therefore, it is recommended that additional confirma-
tion steps be incorporated to enhance its effectiveness.
B. Interpreting Triggering Mechanisms: Implications of Study
Two
Previous research [7], [24] has explored the performance
of using spatial coupling, fixed time, and explicit gestures in
low-density environments. We further discussed how trigger-
ing mechanisms of techniques affect user task performance,
behavior, and experience in complex selection environments,
building on the results of Study Two.
1) Touch (Spatial Coupling): Spatial coupling offers an
intuitive triggering metaphor, achieving near-instant triggering
time. These advantages enable Touch to outperform Dwell-
Time and Pinch in Task Completion Time and Throughput.
Additionally, participants reported that Touch facilitates easier
activation with smaller targets than alternative mechanisms.
This advantage comes from the Touch’s mechanism using only
one coupling point as the trigger [24], eliminating the impact
of the fingers accidentally slipping off the smaller targets.
However, we observed that participants navigated around
obstacles by retracting their fingers when using Touch, as
illustrated in Figure 10a. This finding extends previous studies
[61], which highlighted that ray casting’s lack of disambigua-
tion mechanisms demands extra effort for accurate selections.
Moreover, the expansive interaction space and limited field
of view (FOV) in virtual environments [62] made avoiding
errors outside the focus area challenging, inevitably resulting
in more errors. Therefore, while using spatial coupling alone
as a triggering mechanism is fast, adding additional attributes
as triggering conditions is necessary to achieve more compre-
hensive performance in complex selection environments [2],
[63].
2) Dwell-Time (Spatial Coupling and Fixed Time): Dwell-
Time utilizes a fixed duration of finger-target spatial cou-
pling as an additional triggering condition. This mechanism
effectively filters out potential errors. Moreover, the fixed
time strengthens users’ perception of control over triggering,
allowing direct pointing to the next target without concern
for obstacles (as shown in Figure 10b), leading to shorter
movement distance. However, employing a fixed time signifi-
cantly increases Task Completion Time [7] compared to Touch

3) Pinch (Spatial Coupling and Explicit Gesture): Pinch
employs an explicit gesture as an additional triggering condi-
tion, avoiding a fixed waiting time. According to participant
feedback, explicit gestures significantly heighten awareness
about their engagement, fostering more intentional interactions
than implicit pointing gestures and demonstrating an excep-
tionally low Error Rate.
However, consistent with previous research [7], [64], [65],
the binary gestures extended the interaction time and impaired
users’ perception of the relative spatial positions between their
fingers and the target. This disparity makes it challenging to
ensure the pinch point falls within the target, which is similar
to the Heisenberg Effect of Spatial Interaction [21], [28]
observed in previous device-based techniques, where discrete
input (e.g., button press) disturbs the tracker position, resulting
in a different selection point.
Moreover, the finger movement distance and finger motion
trajectories (Figure 10c) corroborate that participants retract
their fingers when using Pinch. Follow-up interviews revealed
that ergonomic discomfort and intuitive response primarily
caused this phenomenon. P2 and P19 claimed, “ I have to bend
my wrist inward to prevent my fingers from being occluded.
Slightly pulling my hand back when aiming for the next target
helps reduce wrist pain ”. In addition, P4 mentioned, “ Pinch
is more like the natural gesture of grasping objects, so I
instinctively extend my hand forward ”. These findings further
highlight the necessity of considering self-occlusion [65] and
gesture metaphors in designing explicit gestures.
4) Magic-Tap (Spatial Coupling and Kinematic Data):
Magic-Tap utilizes finger kinematic data as an additional
triggering condition. Regarding selection speed, Magic-Tap’s
Task Completion Times and Throughput exhibit no significant
differences from Touch. Specifically, half of the participants
reported perceiving no significant difference in Trigger Time
between Magic-Tap and Touch. However, we identified that
kinematic differences [66] could lead to longer trigger times
for Magic-Tap as the task ID decreases (Figure 8e), potentially
making it challenging for users to achieve precise timing
expectations similar to Dwell-Time. Control theoretic models
of pointing [56] provide a reasonable explanation: conditions
with a lower ID, due to users’ less constrained nature, naturally
result in greater behavioral variability. Unlike the previously
discussed Oscillatory phenomenon, this variability is instead
a reflection of the inherent freedom in user behavior when
performing easier selection tasks, emphasizing an important
consideration for future study.
Regarding Error Rates, Magic-Tap showed no significant
differences compared to Dwell-Time and Pinch, and no errors
were observed across all tasks. In addition, Magic-Tap cancels
activation by detecting an increase in finger speed rather than
spatial decoupling [67], which allows for more responsive
cancellation when interacting with larger targets compared to
Dwell-Time.
Regarding user behavior and experience, Magic-Tap em-
ploys the most natural finger-pointing for triggering [38].
This promotes more accurate pointing and largely prevents
the finger from being occluded [22] and Heisenberg Effect
of Spatial Interaction [28]. Additionally, when participants
utilized Magic-Tap in consecutive selection tasks, they tended
to choose the shortest path directly to the next target (Figure
10d), attributed to its reliable error prevention mechanism.
These advantages significantly reduced the effort required for
participants to complete selection tasks. Consequently, the
NASA-TLX and UEQ results also reflected greater satisfaction
with Magic-Tap’s performance and lower task load than other
techniques. Notably, no differences in learnability were found
between Magic-Tap and well-known techniques such as Touch
and Dwell-Time, further substantiating Magic-Tap’s overall
effectiveness and user-friendliness.
C. Magic-Tap as a Comprehensive Technique
Based on the results and discussions above, we believe that
Magic-Tap embodies the Important Factors previously de-
lineated, making it a comprehensive selection technique with
potentials in various contexts. Magic-Tap, along with Touch
and Dwell-Time, employs an implicit triggering mechanism,
seamlessly integrating pointing and triggering actions, thus
satisfying F1 (Seamlessness). Using the fingertip as an instru-
ment and refining parameters through Study One, Magic-Tap
has become easier and more reliable in triggering, satisfying
F2 (Easy-to-trigger) . Furthermore, the results of Study Two
demonstrate that Magic-Tap’s mechanism maintains a minimal
response time (comparable to Touch) while ensuring reliable
error-proofing (comparable to Dwell-Time and Pinch), thereby
meeting F3 (Error-proofing) and F4 (Temporal Efficiency) .
Benefiting from its natural interaction style and efficient
performance, Magic-Tap aids participants in completing tasks
by promoting motion economy and ergonomic comfort, meet-
ing F5 (Workload) . While no selection technique perfectly
fits every context, Magic-Tap’s unique combination of these
factors provides new insights for further investigations and
an additional option for designers to consider in different
selection tasks.
VII. LIMITATION AND FUTURE WORK
Upon delving into the nuances of virtual hand selection
behaviors and their kinematic profiles, we recognize Magic-
Tap’s current design falls short of perfection. Introducing
the Cancellation Mechanism to address the Snap-back ges-
ture inadvertently impacts Magic-Tap’s responsiveness during
deliberate selection. Future endeavors may aim to enhance
Magic-Tap from a mechanistic standpoint, specifically by
reevaluating and adjusting the kinematic data parameters uti-
lized in its triggering and cancellation processes. Furthermore,
our exploratory tests indicate promising results for Magic-
Tap’s algorithm in mouse operations, though a more thorough
evaluation is required. This has inspired us to use angular
speed and angular acceleration to explore potential applica-
tions in other modalities, such as gaze, head movement, and
ray casting, guiding our future research.
In terms of parameter fine-tuning, our findings indicate
that the response time for selection using kinematic data is
influenced by IDs, which include factors like target size and

VIII. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we present Magic-Tap, an innovative selection
technique in AR/VR that leverages kinematic data from hand
movements to facilitate object triggering. We conducted two
studies: Study One aimed at meticulously calibrating Magic-
Tap’s configurations. Based on the results, we proposed sug-
gestions for optimizing Magic-Tap’s parameter configurations
in various selection tasks. Study Two compared Magic-Tap
with traditional selection techniques (Touch, Dwell-Time, and
Pinch). The results show that Magic-Tap is comparable to
Touch in Task Completion Time and Throughput, while it is
comparable to Dwell-Time and Pinch regarding Error Rate,
and allows users to concentrate exclusively on the selection
task without being distracted by superfluous confirmation
signals. While Touch, Dwell-Time, and Pinch hold irreplace-
able roles in certain specific scenarios, we believe Magic-
Tap represents a novel approach, harnessing kinematic data to
craft intuitive and efficient virtual hand selection techniques
in AR/VR.
